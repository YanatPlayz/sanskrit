\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Our goal is to test whether the standard diachronic-embedding paradigm---train per-period
embeddings, align them, and measure how a word's neighborhood changes \citep{hamilton2016}---%
recovers semantic shifts in Sanskrit that are independently documented by philologists. The
paradigm was developed for modern, word-segmented, higher-resource languages; Sanskrit
violates all three assumptions, so most of our method is the preprocessing and evaluation
needed to make the comparison meaningful. We first normalize and segment the corpus
(\S\ref{sec:prep}), train one frozen-configuration grid of per-period embeddings
(\S\ref{sec:emb}), measure change with alignment-free and alignment-based statistics
(\S\ref{sec:change}), and evaluate recovery against a curated set of documented shifts with a
directional, falsifiable test (\S\ref{sec:recovery}).

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:prep}
Distributional models assume that the input is a sequence of word tokens. Sanskrit hides word
boundaries through \textit{sandhi}, the obligatory phonological fusion of adjacent words---for
example \textit{iti}~`thus' and \textit{uv\=aca}~`said' surface as the single written token
\textit{ityuv\=aca}---and compounds many words into one. Left unsegmented, a single lexeme
scatters across dozens of fused surface forms, fragmenting the very co-occurrence signal an
embedding depends on. Our pipeline therefore prioritizes recovering word boundaries
consistently over any single editorial nicety. Each text passes through six fixed steps:

\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Cleaning.} Remove editorial metadata, verse and chapter markers, and bracketed
        apparatus with regular expressions and manual checks.
  \item \textbf{Transliteration.} Convert to SLP1, a reversible ASCII encoding for Sanskrit
        \citep{slp1}, and apply Unicode NFC normalization, so that orthographically identical
        forms share one byte sequence.
  \item \textbf{Chunking.} Segment into ${\le}350$-character spans at whitespace, to fit the
        context window of the neural analyzer.
  \item \textbf{Sandhi splitting.} Restore word boundaries with ByT5-Sanskrit
        \citep{nehrdich2024byt5}, a byte-level multitask model.
  \item \textbf{Compound refinement.} A second pass re-segments residual long compounds.
  \item \textbf{Lemmatization.} Optionally map forms to lemmas with the same model.
\end{enumerate}

We use a neural segmenter rather than a rule-based one \citep{huet2009heritage} because our
corpus is heterogeneous in genre and orthography, and the byte-level model is robust to forms
absent from any lexicon. We retain three corpora from this pipeline---\textbf{raw} (after
step~3), \textbf{sandhi-split} (after step~5), and \textbf{lemmatized} (after step~6)---and
treat preprocessing as an experimental variable rather than a fixed choice. The effect is
large: sandhi splitting raises the vocabulary trackable across all four periods from 801 to
1{,}345 words ($+68\%$), simply by reuniting fused variants of the same lexeme.

\subsection{Per-period embeddings}
\label{sec:emb}
We train independent embeddings for each period and vary exactly one factor at a time. Every
cell of the grid shares one \emph{frozen} configuration---skip-gram, 300 dimensions, window~5,
minimum count~5, 10 negative samples, subsampling $10^{-4}$, 10 epochs, fixed seed
\citep{mikolov2013,gensim2010}---so that any difference traces to the factor under study and
not to incidental hyperparameters. The grid crosses the three preprocessing variants with two
architectures: \textbf{word2vec}, which has no subword information, and \textbf{FastText}
\citep{bojanowski2017}, which represents a word by its character $n$-grams; for FastText we test
$n$-gram ranges $[3,6]$ and $[6,12]$.

Two configuration choices are themselves empirical findings. First, \textbf{10 epochs}: raising
training to 30 epochs \emph{overtrains} the small period bins (Vedic 349K, Upani\d{s}adic 194K,
S\=utra 227K tokens), sharpening within-period neighbors but degrading the between-period signal
we measure---cross-metric reliability (\S\ref{sec:change}) falls from $0.70$ to $0.25$ for our
primary model, and the flagship \textit{asura} shift recovers less cleanly. Second, the FastText
$n$-gram range is decisive for neighbor quality, as we show in \S\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Measuring change}
\label{sec:change}
Independently trained spaces do not share a coordinate frame, so we align each period onto the
Vedic space with orthogonal Procrustes \citep{hamilton2016}, fitting the rotation on the 767
high-frequency words present in every period. We restrict all quantitative analysis to the
1{,}345 words shared by all four periods---the lexemes we can actually track end to end---and
compute three change statistics per word:

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Second-order similarity} \citep{hamilton2016b}, our primary measure: the cosine
        between a word's vectors of similarities to its neighbors in two periods. It is
        \emph{alignment-free}, so it avoids Procrustes noise.
  \item \textbf{Procrustes cosine}, a confirmatory alignment-based measure: cosine distance
        between a word's aligned vectors in two periods.
  \item \textbf{Nearest-neighbor Jaccard distance}, reported for completeness; we show in
        \S\ref{sec:results} that it saturates on corpora of this size.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluating recovery}
\label{sec:recovery}
Whether the embeddings ``recover'' a documented shift should not be judged by inspecting neighbor
lists alone, which invites confirmation bias. We instead pre-register the prediction for each
shift and test it directionally, following \citet{hamilton2016}.

\paragraph{Validation set.}
We curate 24 documented shifts plus 2 stable controls from \citet{kamboja}, a philological study
of semantic change in Sanskrit. Crucially, the set is selected \emph{blind to our results}: we
take candidate words from Kamboja's index and keep only those frequent enough to track across
periods, before computing any embedding statistic. For each shift we record its source and target
sense and the period transition in which it is documented to occur (e.g.\ \textit{asura}
`lord, mighty god'~$\rightarrow$~`demon', Vedic~$\rightarrow$~Epic).

\paragraph{Directional anchor test.}
For each shift we specify, \emph{a priori from Kamboja's glosses}, small sets of anchor words
denoting the old and the new sense---never drawn from the model's neighbor lists, to keep the
test non-circular. For \textit{asura}, the old-sense anchors are deities (\textit{indra},
\textit{mitra}, \textit{varu\d{n}a}) and the new-sense anchors are demon classes
(\textit{r\=ak\d{s}asa}, \textit{d\=anava}, \textit{daitya}). Writing $c_{\text{old}}(t)$ and
$c_{\text{new}}(t)$ for the cosine similarity of the target to the mean old- and new-sense
anchor in period~$t$, a shift counts as recovered in the attested direction when
\[
\Delta \;=\; \bigl[c_{\text{new}}-c_{\text{old}}\bigr]_{\text{end}}
            -\bigl[c_{\text{new}}-c_{\text{old}}\bigr]_{\text{start}} \;>\; 0
\]
across its documented transition. The two-sided contrast cancels a confound: absolute cosines
shrink across periods as the vocabulary grows, so a one-sided ``moved toward the new sense''
test is biased; the difference of differences removes this common drift. Because four periods
give little power per word, we assess significance over the \emph{population} of shifts with a
binomial sign test (null: direction is a coin flip), and read individual neighbor trajectories
qualitatively as case studies.
